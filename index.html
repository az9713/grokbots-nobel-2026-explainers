The 2026 Nobel Prize in Physics: IceCube and the high-energy neutrino sky

Francis Halzen, sole laureate, 6 Oct 2026

The prize

TakeawayHalzen won for turning a cubic kilometre of Antarctic ice into a telescope, and using it to prove that the universe sends us neutrinos with a billion times the energy of anything from the Sun.

The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences awarded the whole prize to Francis Halzen (University of Wisconsin–Madison) “for decisive contributions to the IceCube Neutrino Observatory and the discovery of high-energy neutrinos of astrophysical origin” [PR]. Established

Halzen was born in 1944 in Tienen, Belgium, and earned his PhD at KU Leuven in 1969 [PR]. In June 1988 he co-wrote, with J. Learned, the first proposal to use ice as both the target and the light-producing medium. He drove the AMANDA prototype and led IceCube as principal investigator from its 1999 NSF proposal onward [SB]. The IceCube collaboration itself is about 450 people at 58 institutions in 14 countries [SB].

The committee was careful about what has not been found. No single point source is established. A small fraction of the flux comes from our own Milky Way, and most comes from extragalactic sources nobody has yet pinned down [SB]. Open

“His tenacity and scientific vision has paved the way for a new kind of astronomy.” Mark Pearce, Chair of the Nobel Committee for Physics [PR]

Neutrinos 101

TakeawayNeutrinos barely interact with anything. That makes them perfect messengers and terrible to catch.

A neutrino (an electrically neutral elementary particle with sub-eV mass) feels only the weak interaction (the force behind radioactive beta decay, carried by the heavy W and Z bosons) and gravity. There are three flavours, νe, νμ and ντ, named for the charged lepton (electron, muon, tau) each one produces when it interacts. Each flavour is a quantum superposition of three mass states, so a neutrino changes flavour as it travels. This is called oscillation [SB].

Why that makes a great messenger

Astronomy has three other carriers from the high-energy sky, and each has a flaw [SB]:

  • Charged cosmic rays get bent by magnetic fields, so they no longer point back to where they came from.
  • Gamma rays get absorbed in dense sources and, over cosmic distances, by background light (the CMB and the extragalactic background light). Electrons can also make them, so seeing gamma rays doesn't prove protons are being accelerated.
  • Neutrinos are neutral and almost never absorbed, so they point straight back and escape from the densest places. A neutrino detection means hadrons (protons, nuclei) were accelerated.

Why it's hard to catch

At 1 PeV (1015 eV) the neutrino–nucleon cross-section (the effective target area each nucleon presents) is about σ ~ 10−33 cm² [SB]. Ice has a nucleon density n ≈ 0.92 g/cm³ × 6.0×10²³ g⁻¹ ≈ 5.5×10²³ cm⁻³, so the mean free path is

λ=1nσ≈1.8×109 cm≈18,000 km ≈ our estimate, order of magnitude, from [SB] inputs

That's longer than Earth's diameter (12,742 km), which is also why PeV neutrinos coming up through the Earth get absorbed. A PeV neutrino crossing 1 km of ice has roughly a 1-in-18,000 chance of interacting ≈ our estimate. Combine that with an astrophysical flux that falls steeply with energy, roughly ∝ E−2.5, and you need a cubic kilometre of detector to collect of order 100 astrophysical events a year [SB].

Cosmic accelerators

TakeawayNeutrinos are the by-product that proves a source accelerates protons, and their energy ties them to gamma rays and cosmic rays.

Cosmic rays were found by Hess in 1912. They are mostly protons and helium nuclei, and reach about 1020 eV, compared with 7 TeV per beam at the LHC [SB]. Their energy spectrum is a broken power law. There's a knee at a few PeV (below it, the sources are Galactic, probably supernova remnants) and an ankle at a few EeV (above it, the flux is extragalactic, about one particle per km² per year). Above about 50 EeV comes the GZK cutoff, where cosmic rays lose energy on CMB photons [SB].

Particles gain energy by bouncing back and forth across shock fronts, which is called first-order Fermi acceleration. The Hillas criterion says a source must be big and magnetised enough to hold on to a particle until it reaches that energy [SB].

From protons to neutrinos

When those protons hit gas (pp) or photons (pγ), they make pions [SB]:

p+p→π0,π±+X p+γ→Δ+(1232)→pπ0 or nπ+ π0→γγπ+→μ+νμ,μ+→e+νeν‾μ

So every hadronic accelerator makes gamma rays and neutrinos together.

Their energy fluxes are linked, with the neutrino side counting all flavours [SB], [HK]:

Eγ2dNγdEγ≈43KπEν2dNνdEν|Eν=Eγ/2,Kπ≈2 (pp), ≈1 (pγ) Eν side summed over all neutrino flavours · prefactor 4/(3Kπ) checked against [SB] p. 4

A rule of thumb: in photoproduction the pion takes about 20% of the proton's energy, a gamma ray about 1/10, and a neutrino about 1/20. So PeV–EeV protons give tens-of-TeV to tens-of-PeV neutrinos, which is exactly IceCube's range [SB]. Pion decay produces flavours in the ratio νe:νμ:ντ ≈ 1:2:0. Oscillation over cosmic distances evens that out to about 1:1:1 by the time they reach Earth (standard physics, not stated in [SB]).

The detector

TakeawayIceCube is 5,160 light sensors frozen into a cubic kilometre of the clearest ice on Earth, 1.5 to 2.5 km under the South Pole.

Seawater detectors came first and struggled. DUMAND, 4800 m deep off Hawaii, deployed one string in 1993, it failed, and the project was cancelled in 1995. Ocean bioluminescence and radioactive ⁴⁰K were background problems [SB]. Halzen's bet was ice. Sensors lowered into Greenland boreholes in 1991 showed it was transparent enough. The AMANDA prototype (1993–2000) then found that air bubbles at 800–1000 m ruin the light, cutting the scattering length below 50 cm. So IceCube went deeper, where the ice has an absorption length of ~200 m and a scattering length of ~50 m [SB].

IceCube was proposed in 1999, funded in March 2002, and its strings were deployed from 2005 to 2010 in holes melted with hot water. It was complete in 2011 and has run with more than 99% uptime since [SB].

The geometry [SB]: 86 vertical strings, each holding 60 DOMs (digital optical modules: a pressure sphere holding a downward-facing 25.4 cm photomultiplier tube that digitises its own signals). DOMs sit every 17 m between 1450 and 2450 m deep. Strings sit on a hexagonal grid 125 m apart. That makes 5,160 DOMs ≈ our count; [SB] says “over 5000” watching about 1 km³, roughly a gigatonne of ice. IceTop, on the surface, catches air showers from 300 TeV to 1 EeV and helps veto them.

How IceCube sees: Cherenkov light

Cherenkov light is emitted when a charged particle moves faster than light does in the medium (v > c/n). It comes out as a cone of mostly UV light, much like a sonic boom. In water (n = 1.33) the threshold is 0.75c, which for a muon means 160 MeV [SB]. Ice has n ≈ 1.31 [SB], so:

Reading an event

TakeawayA muon track gives you direction. A cascade gives you energy. Choosing which events to trust is the whole game.

Above a TeV, a neutrino interacts by deep inelastic scattering (it smashes into a quark inside a nucleon) [SB]. In a charged-current interaction (W exchange) it turns into its charged lepton plus a spray of hadrons, νℓ + N → ℓ + X. In a neutral-current one (Z exchange) it only leaves the hadron spray, ν + N → ν + X. Two shapes come out [SB]:

TrackCascade
Made byCC νμ (a muon)CC νe, ντ, and NC of all flavours
Looks likea line crossing the array (km long at TeV)a near-spherical blob (shower < 10 m)
Direction0.3° at 100 TeV~5° above 50 TeV
Energywithin a factor of ~2~8% at 100 TeV

Direction comes from photon arrival times, and energy from the number of photons [SB]. A ντ can make a double bang: one cascade where it interacts and a second where the tau decays, about 50 m apart per PeV [SB].

Backgrounds

Cosmic-ray air showers send down atmospheric muons at about 3 kHz [SB]. Two tricks remove them. One is to keep only up-going tracks, since the Earth stops muons, which cuts the rate to the µHz level. The other is to use the outer layer of sensors as a veto and keep only events that start inside a ~500 Mt inner volume.

That still leaves about 100,000 atmospheric neutrinos a year, with a steep spectrum ∝ E−3.7. The astrophysical spectrum, ∝ E−2.5, wins above a few tens of TeV and gives about 100 events a year [SB].

The discovery

TakeawayTwo accidental PeV events in 2010–12 data (published 2013) opened the door. By 2014 the astrophysical signal was at 5.7σ, and it now spans 5 TeV to beyond 10 PeV.

While searching data from May 2010 to May 2012 for much higher-energy (EeV) neutrinos, IceCube found two cascades at about 1.04 and 1.14 PeV, nicknamed Bert and Ernie (2010–12 data, published 2013) [SB], [PRL2013]; nicknames from IceCube outreach. A dedicated search using the veto found 28 events from 30 TeV to 1.14 PeV, 21 cascades and 7 tracks [S2013]. Three years of data then ruled out a purely atmospheric origin at 5.7σ [PRL2014]. Here σ means standard deviations, and 5σ is physics' discovery bar. Established

The picture today [SB], [SPEC26] comes from a combined fit of 231,486 tracks and 10,569 cascades recorded between May 2010 and May 2021. Above ~30 TeV the spectrum is a power law, below that it flattens, and a single power law from 5 TeV to 10 PeV is rejected at >4σ. The arrival directions are isotropic, as expected if most sources are far outside the Galaxy. The highest-energy IceCube event is 11.4 PeV, recorded on 31 March 2019 and published in 2026.

The neutrino energy flux is comparable to the gamma-ray and cosmic-ray fluxes, which suggests they share sources Open. Seven tau neutrinos (20 TeV–1 PeV) rule out a zero astrophysical tau flux at 5σ, confirming that the flux has oscillated across cosmic distances [TAU] Established.

Where they come from

TakeawayThe Milky Way is a confirmed but small source. Two active galaxies are strong evidence. Most of the flux is still unexplained.

The Milky Way Established

4.5σ in 2023 [GAL23], and 5.7σ with 12 years of data in 2026 [GAL26], arXiv preprint. The signal matches the gamma-ray glow cosmic rays make on interstellar gas. It is the first neutrino source above 5σ, but it is diffuse, with no point sources, and it is a small fraction of the total [SB].

TXS 0506+056, a blazar Evidence

A blazar is an active galaxy whose jet points at us. On 22 Sep 2017 a ~290 TeV neutrino, IceCube-170922A, landed within 0.06° of it while it was flaring in gamma rays at about 7× its usual brightness. The alert went out within a minute, and telescopes worldwide followed up. The archive then showed a neutrino flare from Sep 2014 to Mar 2015 at 3.5σ [SB] [TXS-MM] [TXS-ARCH].

NGC 1068, a Seyfert galaxy Evidence

A Seyfert galaxy is an active galaxy with a buried, jetless black hole, here about 15 million solar masses and 46 million light-years away. IceCube saw 79 neutrinos at 1–10 TeV, at 4.2σ. Gamma rays are far weaker than the neutrinos, so the neutrinos likely come from the hot corona near the black hole, where gamma rays get absorbed [SB] [NGC].

Gamma-ray bursts Ruled out as dominant

More than 1000 searches have found no neutrinos coincident with gamma-ray bursts [SB].
“Time will tell if these observations confirm AGNs as sources.” Nobel Committee for Physics [SB]

What it means, what's next

TakeawayA new kind of astronomy exists. The next decade is about finding its sources, and using it as a particle collider that nature runs for free.

Established

  • A diffuse astrophysical flux exists
  • It extends past 10 PeV
  • It is mostly extragalactic and isotropic
  • The Galaxy contributes, at 5.7σ
  • Astrophysical tau neutrinos, at 5σ
  • The spectrum is not a single power law (>4σ)

Evidence

  • TXS 0506+056 (blazar)
  • NGC 1068 (Seyfert)

Open

  • Which source populations dominate
  • AGN core or jet
  • pp or pγ
  • GRB contribution (none seen)
  • The prompt charm flux
  • The link to ultra-high-energy cosmic rays

What's next

The next step is telescopes bigger than a cubic kilometre: IceCube-Gen2 at the South Pole, KM3NeT in the Mediterranean, P-ONE off Vancouver Island, TRIDENT in the South China Sea, and Baikal-GVD [SB]. IceCube's real-time alerts go out within a minute, which makes it a trigger for multi-messenger astronomy (observing one event with neutrinos, light, and possibly gravitational waves) [SB].

Open Neutrinos can also do particle physics at extreme energies. Earth's absorption of high-energy neutrinos could be used to measure cross-sections at collision energies beyond the LHC, and flavour ratios can be tested for new physics [SB].
