The 2026 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine: switching neurons on and off with light

Karl Deisseroth, Peter Hegemann, Georg Nagel · 5 Oct 2026

The prize

TakeawayAn alga's light sensor turned out to be a light-switched pore, and putting it into neurons let scientists turn chosen brain cells on and off with millisecond timing.

The Nobel Assembly at Karolinska Institutet split the prize equally among three laureates “for their discoveries concerning light-gated ion channels and optogenetics” [PR]. Established

  • Karl Deisseroth (HHMI and Stanford, born 1971)
  • Peter Hegemann (Humboldt University of Berlin, born 1954)
  • Georg Nagel (University of Würzburg, born 1953)

In the press release's framing, Hegemann and Nagel discovered channelrhodopsin (a light-sensing protein from algae that is itself an ion channel), and Deisseroth turned it into a light-controlled switch for neurons: first in cultured neurons in 2005, then in living mice in 2007 [PR]. The prize is 12 million SEK, shared equally [PR].

“Optogenetics provides opportunities for mapping the brain in a way that we could once only dream of.” Per Svenningsson, committee chair [PR]

The minimum neuroscience

TakeawayNeurons compute with millisecond voltage spikes, so a useful control tool has to be that fast and has to hit only one cell type.

A neuron is a cell that signals electrically. Its interior is held negative relative to the outside; this is the resting potential, typically around −70 mV (textbook approximation, not from [SB]). Pushing the membrane less negative is depolarization. Past a threshold it fires an action potential (a spike), an all-or-none pulse about a millisecond long (textbook approximation, not from [SB]). Pushing it more negative is hyperpolarization, which suppresses spikes. [SB] stresses that the brain computes “on the millisecond timescale” [SB].

Ions cross the membrane in two ways [SB pp. 6, 8]:

  • An ion channel is a gated pore. When open, ions flow passively down their gradient, many per opening, and fast.
  • A pump actively pushes ions against their gradient. Light-driven pumps move one ion per photon, so their currents are smaller.

This difference explains why channelrhodopsin, a light-gated channel (tab 4), mattered so much.

A rhodopsin is a protein that crosses the membrane seven times and holds retinal, a light-absorbing molecule related to vitamin A. Light changes retinal's shape, and that changes the protein. In microbial rhodopsins, the protein moves ions directly. Animal vision instead relays the signal through a slower chain of enzymes [SB].

Genetic targeting: a virus delivers the gene with a promoter, a DNA switch that only one cell type reads. Only those cells make the protein. The method in one line: the protein picks which cells; the light picks where and when.

Francis Crick had asked for exactly this. He wanted a way to switch “all neurons of just one type” while “leaving the others more or less unaltered”, and he wrote that “the ideal signal would be light”, an idea he called “rather far-fetched” [SB] [Crick 1999].

The problem: earlier attempts

TakeawayEvery earlier light-control trick failed at least one requirement: genetically targetable, one component, fast, and needing no added chemical.

The criteria [SB] set for an ideal tool [SB]:

  • genetically encodable and compact
  • expresses well in the membrane
  • fast and reversible
  • turns photons directly into current

[SB]'s conclusion: the answer had to be “a single protein… delivered genetically… coupl[ing] photon absorption directly to ionic current with millisecond kinetics” [SB].

The discovery: from alga to channel

TakeawayA photocurrent too fast for an enzyme chain suggested that receptor and pore were one protein, and putting the gene into frog eggs proved it.

  1. The alga. Chlamydomonas reinhardtii is a single-celled green alga that swims toward light using an eyespot, a light-sensing patch [SB].
  2. The clue (1991). Harz and Hegemann recorded currents from swimming cells with a suction pipette. The current started microseconds after a flash of light. That is too fast for a chain of messenger molecules, so Hegemann's team proposed that the light receptor and the ion channel are a single protein [Harz & Hegemann 1991] [SB]. Established
  3. The genes. Three groups (Hegemann, Spudich, Takahashi) independently found algal genes resembling bacteriorhodopsin. Bacteriorhodopsin is a light-driven proton pump found in 1971 in a salt-loving microbe [Oesterhelt & Stoeckenius 1971], and Hegemann trained in the lab that studied it. He named the genes Chop1 and Chop2. Spudich's group showed that they drive the photocurrents [Sineshchekov 2002], and Takahashi's group located them in the eyespot [SB]. [SB] credits all three groups for this step.
  4. ChR1 (2002). Nagel, Hegemann and Bamberg expressed the gene in Xenopus oocytes, large frog egg cells used as test tubes for membrane proteins. Voltage clamp holds the cell's voltage fixed and measures the current. Green light drove an inward proton current. A shortened core of the protein was enough, so the protein is the channel. This was the first light-gated ion channel [Nagel 2002] [SB]. Established
  5. ChR2 (2003). It responds to bluer light and passes cations in general, meaning positive ions, not only protons. The first 315 amino acids suffice. Under steady light the current spikes to a peak, then settles to a plateau [Nagel 2003] [SB]. [SB] calls this “arguably more consequential”, because letting positive ions in depolarizes a neuron. Established

Making neurons obey light

TakeawayIn 2005, ChR2 put into neurons made them fire on cue, pulse for pulse, and in 2007 the same trick moved a living mouse's whiskers.

2005 (Boyden, Zhang, Bamberg, Nagel, Deisseroth). The team used a lentivirus, a gene-delivery virus, to put ChR2 into cultured hippocampal neurons from rats [PR]; [SB] does not name the species. The protein sat in the membrane with no apparent toxicity. Blue light pulses drove precise, millisecond-scale spikes, including trains at increasing frequencies [Boyden 2005] [SB]. No retinal had to be added, because the trace amount already in the cells was enough. That detail made in-animal work practical. Established

Within a year, other groups confirmed it in chick spinal cord, worms (C. elegans), retina, fruit flies and living tissue [Li 2005] [Nagel 2005] [Bi 2006] [Zhang 2006] [SB].

2006. Deisseroth and co-authors coined the word optogenetics in a J Neurosci review [Deisseroth 2006]; his ten-year retrospective tells the story [Deisseroth 2015].

2007 (Aravanis et al.). A laser diode was coupled to an optical fibre implanted in the brain of freely moving rodents. Light on ChR2-expressing neurons in the whisker motor cortex made the whiskers deflect [SB] [Aravanis 2007]. Established

The toolbox

TakeawayAfter the on-switch came off-switches and colour variants, so one experiment could excite and silence different cells with different colours.

  • Silencing (2007). NpHR is a yellow-light chloride pump from a salt-loving microbe. It pushes negative ions in, which hyperpolarizes the neuron. Because it responds to a different colour from blue ChR2, the same tissue could be driven both ways: the first two-colour, bidirectional control [Zhang 2007] [Han & Boyden 2007] [SB]. Its limit is that, as a pump, it moves only one ion per photon. Established
  • Chloride channels. ChR2 was re-engineered to pass chloride [Berndt 2014] [Wietek 2014]. Natural anion channels GtACR1 and GtACR2 came from the alga Guillardia theta [Govorunova 2015]. They have much higher light sensitivity and chloride conductance than the pumps [SB].
  • Red-shifted. Chrimson responds to longer, redder light and is used in primate and human retina [SB].
  • Chemogenetics, the complementary approach. DREADDs are engineered receptors switched on by a drug rather than light [Armbruster 2007]. They are slower and act body-wide, which suits long-lasting modulation [SB].

What it revealed

TakeawayFor the first time, researchers could write activity into a defined circuit and watch the behaviour change, which proves cause instead of correlation.

[SB]'s thesis is that behaviours “are assembled from discrete, projection-defined circuits”, and that neuroscience moved “from observing and ‘reading’… to actively ‘writing’” [SB]. Here a projection means the axon pathway from one brain region to another. All of the findings below are Established in mice.

Sleep → waking

Stimulating hypocretin (orexin) neurons in the lateral hypothalamus (LH) made the mouse more likely to wake from both deep and REM sleep, and how fast depended on stimulation frequency. This was one of the first direct causal links to a mammalian behaviour [Adamantidis 2007] [SB].

Memory engrams

An engram is the set of cells that stores a memory. Cells in the hippocampal dentate gyrus (DG) that were active during fear learning were tagged with ChR2. Reactivating them in a neutral box made mice freeze, so these cells are sufficient for recall. The same method planted a false memory: the safe-box cells were reactivated during a shock, and the mice then feared the safe box [Liu 2012] [Ramirez 2013].

Reward vs aversion

In the VTA, a dopamine hub, input from the laterodorsal tegmentum (LDT) drives reward and input from the lateral habenula (LHb) drives aversion. Dopamine is not one broadcast signal [Lammel 2012].

Anxiety

Basolateral amygdala → central amygdala (BLA → CeA) lowers anxiety, basolateral amygdala → ventral hippocampus (BLA → vHPC) raises it, and the BNST (bed nucleus of the stria terminalis, a forebrain hub tied to anxiety) splits into risk-avoidance (→ LH) and positive-valence (→ VTA) outputs [Tye 2011] [SB].

Aggression and parenting

Esr1+ neurons (neurons that make oestrogen receptor alpha, the marker used to target them) in the VMH (ventromedial hypothalamus) give a graded continuum from social interaction to mounting to attack as intensity rises. Galanin neurons in the medial preoptic area (MPOA) split parenting into separate modules [Lin 2011] [Kohl 2018] [SB].

Locomotion

Neurons in the MLR (mesencephalic locomotor region, a midbrain area that drives movement) separate into streams for speed, body extension and forelimb use [SB].

The clinic, what's open, sources

TakeawayOne blind patient has partial vision back. That is a proof of concept, not yet a therapy.

Restoring vision [SB]

  • The disease. In retinitis pigmentosa, inherited mutations kill the rods and cones, the retina's light-sensing cells. The retinal ganglion cells, which carry the signal to the brain, survive.
  • The idea. Make those surviving ganglion cells light-sensitive.
  • The path to people. It was tested in mice with ChR2 [Bi 2006], then in marmosets, then in macaques with Chrimson [SB].
  • The first patient [Sahel 2021]: Chrimson was delivered into the ganglion cells, and engineered goggles turned the scene into light pulses. The patient regained prolonged, partial vision and could tell apart high-contrast objects on a table. EEG, a recording of brain activity from scalp electrodes, showed object-related activity in visual cortex. Evidence (n = 1)
  • Trials. [SB] says “several clinical trials… are presently underway” [SB]. [SB] gives no names or numbers.

Established

  • ChR1 and ChR2 are single-protein light-gated channels (2002, 2003).
  • ChR2 drives millisecond spikes in neurons (2005).
  • The method controls behaviour in vivo (2007).
  • Causal circuit-behaviour links for sleep, memory, reward, anxiety, aggression, parenting and locomotion.

Evidence

  • Human vision restoration rests on one patient [Sahel 2021], with trials underway [SB].

Open

  • Wider human therapy. The hurdles are getting light into tissue, gene-therapy safety and immune responses. Our interpretation; not discussed in [SB].
  • [SB] says chemogenetics “may eventually” be usable in humans [SB].
