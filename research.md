## 1. The prize (FACT, [PR])
- Announced **7 October 2026** by the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences. The laureates:
  - **Henri B. Kagan**: born 1930 in Boulogne-Billancourt, France. PhD 1960, Collège de France. Professor Emeritus at the then Université Paris-Sud.
  - **Kenso Soai**: born 1950 in Hiroshima, Japan. PhD 1979, University of Tokyo. Professor Emeritus at Tokyo University of Science.
- The citation reads: "**for the discovery of non-linear effects and autocatalysis in asymmetric organic synthesis**." The prize is 12 million SEK, shared equally.
- The committee chair, Heiner Linke, said: "Henri Kagan and Kenso Soai have provided a solution to a chemical mystery that is over a century old: how homochirality can emerge spontaneously. The chemical reactions they have developed are spectacular."
- The press release headline is "They made chemistry choose a mirror image." Its milestones are Kagan 1986, Soai's key publication in 1995, and 2003, when Soai "finally succeeded."
- Wording differences: [SB]'s first sentence says "non-linear effects in asymmetric catalysis and asymmetric autocatalysis," while the official citation says "asymmetric organic synthesis." **Use the official citation word for word.**

## 2. Backgrounder: the minimum chemistry (definitions are FACT from the [SB] footnotes unless tagged)
- **Chirality:** a molecule that can't be superimposed on its mirror image, like a left and right hand. The two mirror forms are **enantiomers** (fn 4). They have identical physical properties in a non-chiral environment but interact differently with other chiral things, such as polarised light, enzymes and receptors. *(The last clause is textbook, DERIVED.)*
- **R/S** labels the absolute configuration at a stereocentre with four different substituents (fn 16). **D/L** is the older convention used for sugars and amino acids. Life uses **L-amino acids and D-sugars** ([SB] p. 2).
- **Racemate:** a 50:50 mixture. **Scalemic:** any non-racemic mixture (fn 34). **Homochiral:** all molecules have the same handedness (fn 2).
- **Enantiomeric excess** (fn 1): \( ee = |R-S|/(R+S) \times 100\% \). So 90% ee means 95:5. *(DERIVED: \(R/(R+S) = (1+ee)/2\).)*
- **Asymmetric catalysis:** a chiral catalyst, typically a metal M plus a chiral ligand L*, makes one enantiomer faster. Earlier Nobels covered this: 2001 for asymmetric catalysis and 2021 for asymmetric organocatalysis ([SB] p. 3).
- **Autocatalysis:** a product that catalyses its own formation. [SB]'s example: acetic acid catalyses the hydrolysis of ethyl acetate, which produces acetic acid.
- **Diastereomers:** stereoisomers that are not mirror images. Unlike enantiomers, they *do* differ in energy and reactivity. This is the physical reason an R·R complex and an R·S complex behave differently, which is the key to Kagan's insight. *(Textbook, DERIVED.)*
- Why chemists care: in drugs, often only one enantiomer has the desired effect ([PR]; [POP] p. 1).
- Abiotic synthesis without a chiral influence "invariably" gives racemates ([SB] p. 2). So where did life's homochirality come from? The open question is what process could create and amplify an initial imbalance ([SB] p. 2–3).

## 3. Frank's 1953 model: the theory the laureates made real (FACT [SB] p. 3)
- F. Charles Frank, a theoretical physicist at Bristol, described "a chemical substance which is a catalyst for its own production and an anti-catalyst for the production of its optical antimer."
- Three criteria: **(i) autocatalysis, (ii) enantioselective catalysis, (iii) mutual antagonism (inhibition).** Frank closed with: "A laboratory demonstration may not be impossible."
- Before Kagan and Soai, (i) and (ii) were each known, but neither (iii) nor the combination of (i) and (ii) had been described ([SB] p. 3).
- Schematic kinetics, **DERIVED** (my illustrative form, not Frank's notation): with achiral feedstock A, \( \dot R = k\,A\,R - \mu\,R\,S \) and \( \dot S = k\,A\,S - \mu\,R\,S \). The difference \( R-S \) grows as \( (R-S)\,kA \), but so does the total, so copying alone leaves ee unchanged. With \(ee=(R-S)/(R+S)\), the result is \( \dot{ee} = \tfrac{\mu}{2}(R+S)\,ee\,(1-ee^2) \): **only the mutual-antagonism term amplifies ee** (corrected per Cleo; checked algebraically). **Flag 9:** if this goes in, label it as an illustrative form.
- Caveat in [SB]'s own words (FACT, p. 4): the Frank model "is not an answer to the origin of biological homochirality". It is one possible solution among several, for an event about 3.5–4 billion years ago.

## 4. Kagan 1986: non-linear effects (NLE)
**Linear dogma** (FACT [SB] p. 5, Eq. 1): \( ee_{prod} = ee_{max} \times ee_{ligand} \), where \(ee_{max}\) is the product ee from an enantiopure ligand. This holds for ML*, a metal with one chiral ligand.

**Kagan's insight** (FACT [SB] p. 6): if the active catalyst carries **two** ligands (ML*₂), a scalemic ligand pool gives homochiral \(ML_RL_R\) and \(ML_SL_S\) plus a heterochiral, **meso-like \(ML_RL_S\)**, which is a diastereomer with different reactivity.
- If the heterochiral complex is **less** reactive, product ee is **higher** than linear: **+NLE, asymmetric amplification.**
- If the heterochiral complex is **more** reactive, product ee is **lower**: **–NLE.** The ± naming came from Kagan and, independently, Mikami (fn 33).
- Kagan's model (FACT [SB] Eqs. 2–3): \( ee_{prod} = ee_{max}\cdot ee_{ligand}\cdot f \), with \( f = (1+\beta)/(1+g\beta) \). Here **β** is the ratio of meso to homochiral catalyst, and **g** is the relative reactivity of the heterochiral to the homochiral catalyst. *(The PDF text layer drops the Greek letter; the glyph is β, verified in the raw PDF as U+F062 in the Symbol font. [SB] p. 8 says "large g values" means a much more reactive heterochiral complex, which fixes g = k_hetero/k_homo.)*
- **DERIVED** from Eq. 3: g = 1 gives f = 1 (linear); g < 1 gives f > 1 (+NLE); g > 1 gives f < 1 (–NLE). If the meso complex forms irreversibly and is inert, product ee equals \(ee_{max}\) at any ligand ee (FACT [SB] p. 7: the "interesting corollary").
- **Worked example** ([POP] Fig. 4 gives the numbers; I verified the arithmetic, DERIVED): with a 75:25 ligand (ee 50%) and random pairing, RR = 0.75² = 56.25%, SS = 0.25² = 6.25%, and RS = 2·0.75·0.25 = 37.5%. If RS "barely works", the active pool is 56.25 : 6.25 = 90 : 10, so ee 80%. In Kagan's terms that's β = 0.375/0.625 = 0.6, and with g = 0, f = 1.6, giving 0.5 × 1.6 = 0.8. ✔ This assumes statistical complex formation, which [POP] calls "randomly".
- **The 1986 experiments** (FACT [SB] p. 6): sulfide-to-sulfoxide oxidation with Ti(O-i-Pr)₄, (+)-DET, H₂O and t-BuOOH gave –NLE. **Sharpless epoxidation of geraniol gave +NLE** ([SB] Fig. 2b; the original plot is from ref. 20). The L-proline-catalysed Hajos–Parrish reaction gave –NLE (but see flag 6).
- Later extensions (FACT [SB]):
  - ML₃ and ML₄ complexes, and the **reservoir effect**, where an inactive heterochiral complex removes ligand (1994).
  - **Hyperpositive NLE**, where product ee exceeds \(ee_{max}\). The only example so far: Bellemin-Laponnaz 2020, Me₂Zn plus PhCHO with (–)-NBE. Product ee peaks at **53%** at a ligand ee of just **5%**, then falls to **16%** at a ligand ee of 100%, because a racemic meso dimer precipitates.
  - Blackmond's kinetic models: rate and product ee vary with conversion (1997–2000).
  - Proline under phase control: the racemate precipitates and the solution sits at a eutectic of **about 50% ee**, giving +NLE below 50% ligand ee and –NLE above (Klussmann 2006; independently Hayashi 2006).
  - **Chiral poisoning** (Faller) and chiral activation (Mikami 1997).
- **Showcase numbers** (FACT [SB]):
  - **Noyori's DAIB:** diethylzinc plus benzaldehyde, with 15% ee ligand, gives **98%** ee product. This is the reservoir mechanism: the heterochiral dinuclear Zn dimer is thermodynamically more stable and traps the minor enantiomer, and the active catalyst is the monomer.
  - Oguni 1988 found a strong +NLE in the same reaction.
  - **Merck (L-699,392, a leukotriene antagonist):** α-pinene of 70% optical purity gives a ketone reduction product of 95% ee (stoichiometric Ipc₂BCl).
  - Jacobsen's (salen)Cr epoxide opening: an NLE revealed **cooperative two-metal catalysis.**
- Why it matters (FACT [SB] p. 7, 9, 16): NLE became a **diagnostic tool for mechanism**, showing how many chiral units sit in the enantio-determining step, and a design principle.

## 5. Soai: asymmetric autocatalysis with amplification
- **Why plain autocatalysis isn't enough** (FACT [SB] p. 10): if each cycle copies chirality imperfectly, ee erodes. With ee_max 90%, the sequence runs 100% → 90% → 81% → … To amplify, the catalyst must also be Frank's "anti-catalyst" for its antimer, which means +NLE.
- **Precursors** (FACT): Alberts & Wynberg 1989 showed "enantioselective autoinduction" (ligand ee 70% gave product ee 32%), though the product isn't the catalyst. Wynberg speculated that the Oguni +NLE system could be turned autocatalytic.
- **1990 (Soai, Niwa & Hori):** i-Pr₂Zn plus pyridine-3-carbaldehyde, catalysed by the product's own zinc alkoxide. It was autocatalytic and enantioselective, but ee **eroded**: catalyst ee 86%, product ee 35%. That meets two of Frank's three criteria.
- **1995 (Soai et al., Nature):** i-Pr₂Zn plus pyrimidine-5-carbaldehyde, catalysed by the product, a 5-pyrimidyl alkanol (S)-4. Starting from **5% ee**, run 1 gave **55%**, run 2 gave **87%**, and runs 3–5 plateaued at **90%** ([SB] Fig. 7, entries 1–5). **All three Frank criteria are met.** The reaction is now called the **Soai reaction.**
- **2003 (Sato … Soai, Angew):** a 2-alkynylpyrimidyl variant, from **0.00005% ee to >99.5% ee in three consecutive runs**. [SB] describes this as an "amplification of the stereoisomeric ratio by a factor of 630,000." [POP] Fig. 6 gives the per-run values 0.00005% → 57% → 99% → >99.5%.
  - DERIVED: >99.5% ee means >99.75 : 0.25.
  - **Flag 4:** the 630,000 factor can't be reproduced from the two ee values alone (99.5/0.00005 ≈ 2×10⁶), so it must use a definition [SB] doesn't give. Quote it and don't recompute it.
- **Absolute asymmetric synthesis**, meaning no chiral input at all (FACT [SB] p. 13): Soai (2003, R = t-BuC≡C) and **Singleton** (2002/03, R = Me) independently showed it. Racemic zinc alkoxide carries a statistical fluctuation, which Mislow called "cryptochirality," and the +NLE amplifies it. **In 37 runs, 18 gave R and 19 gave S, with product ee from 15% to 91%.** The handedness is a coin flip and the amplification is deterministic. This is symmetry breaking.
  - DERIVED, illustrative: for N ≈ 6×10²⁰ molecules (1 mmol), the typical random excess is about \(\sqrt N\), which is a fractional ee around 4×10⁻¹¹. That is far below what's measurable, yet enough to seed the outcome. Label it as an illustration; [SB] gives no such number.
  - DERIVED: 18 vs 19 is consistent with p = 0.5.
- Other triggers (FACT [SB] p. 12): circularly polarised light acting on racemates, chiral minerals, crystals and surfaces, and isotope chirality can all set the outcome.
- Limits (FACT [SB] p. 13–14): it is the **only** known asymmetric autocatalysis that both amplifies and achieves absolute asymmetric synthesis, and it has a narrow substrate scope. It is "not relevant for the emergence of biological homochirality in aqueous systems". Current origin-of-life work focuses on network processes and kinetic resolution (Deng & Blackmond 2024).

## 6. The Soai mechanism: still debated (FACT [SB] p. 14–16, timeline)
| Year | Who | Proposal |
|---|---|---|
| 1990 | Soai | a zinc monoalkoxide catalyst |
| 2001 | Blackmond & Brown | reaction calorimetry supports a **dimeric** catalyst |
| 2003 | Buono & Blackmond | kinetics points to a **tetrameric** transition state, predicting rate and NLE |
| 2007 | Klankermayer, Gridnev & Brown (DFT) | a **square-macrocycle-square (SMS)** end product |
| 2010 | Quaranta, Brown & Blackmond | **inverse temperature dependence** of rate; active catalyst derived from SMS |
| 2015 | Matsumoto, Kawasaki & Soai | **X-ray structures** of homo- and heterochiral tetramers: two Zn₂O₂ squares linked through N→Zn into a 12-membered macrocycle, with two unsaturated Zn Lewis-acid sites |
| 2020 | Athavale, Simon, Houk & Denmark | the aldehyde binds the homochiral SMS tetramer "floor-to-floor" at two unsaturated Zn sites, which is geometrically disfavoured in the heterochiral tetramer. Consistent with Kagan and Frank |
| 2020–25 | Trapp et al. (in situ HRMS) | a transient **zinc hemiacetal** (product–substrate) catalyst. Amplification is a **kinetically controlled non-equilibrium stationary state**, explicitly *different* from the Kagan/Frank picture |
- Status (FACT [SB] p. 16): "Two detailed mechanisms have been put forward." They might be reconciled because they studied different substrates (Geiger 2022). **Tag this Open.**

## 7. Applications (FACT unless tagged)
- NLE is a routine **mechanistic diagnostic** and design tool for asymmetric catalysis ([SB] p. 3, 16; [POP] p. 6).
- Pharmaceutical process chemistry: the Merck L-699,392 example (§4). **Carreira's efavirenz** (anti-HIV) intermediate uses an asymmetric autocatalytic step, with "no details of NLE reported" ([SB] p. 14).
- [POP] p. 6 says this matters for pharmaceuticals, flavours, scents, agrochemicals and materials. [PR] says "decisive for chemists who design reactions for the manufacture of pharmaceuticals." These are general claims with no further examples in [SB]. **SPEC** beyond the two named cases.
- Origin of life: the Soai reaction is a **proof of concept** that homochirality *can* arise from achiral starting materials in a closed system. It is not the actual prebiotic route ([SB] p. 4, 13).

## 8. Fact vs speculation
**Established:** product ee can deviate non-linearly from catalyst ee, in both directions, when higher-order or aggregated catalysts are involved (Kagan 1986, plus many examples). Asymmetric autocatalysis with amplification exists (Soai 1995). Amplification from 0.00005% to >99.5% ee happened in three runs (2003). Absolute asymmetric synthesis with roughly 50:50 random handedness has been shown (Soai and Singleton).
**Evidence (partial):** the specific Soai mechanism. There are two competing detailed models (Denmark's SMS tetramer vs Trapp's hemiacetal), which might be substrate-dependent.
**Open or speculative:** how biological homochirality actually arose. [SB] says "we will probably never have a definitive answer." Also open: a Soai-like reaction for amino acids or sugars in water ([POP] p. 6 says groups are trying), and broader industrial use of autocatalysis.
