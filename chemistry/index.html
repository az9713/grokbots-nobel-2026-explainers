The 2026 Nobel Prize in Chemistry: how chemistry learned to choose a mirror image

Henri B. Kagan and Kenso Soai, shared equally, 7 Oct 2026

The prize

TakeawayKagan showed that a catalyst's handedness can be amplified rather than diluted, and Soai built a molecule that copies its own handedness until one mirror form takes over, the first lab demonstration of a 1953 idea about how life became one-handed.

On 7 October 2026 the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences split the prize equally between Henri B. Kagan (born 1930, Boulogne-Billancourt; Professor Emeritus, then Université Paris-Sud) and Kenso Soai (born 1950, Hiroshima; Professor Emeritus, Tokyo University of Science) “for the discovery of non-linear effects and autocatalysis in asymmetric organic synthesis” [PR]. The prize is 12 million SEK [PR].

“Henri Kagan and Kenso Soai have provided a solution to a chemical mystery that is over a century old: how homochirality can emerge spontaneously. The chemical reactions they have developed are spectacular.” Heiner Linke, committee chair [PR]

The one-paragraph version. Many molecules come in left- and right-handed forms, and life uses only one of each. Ordinary chemistry from non-handed ingredients always gives a 50:50 mix. Kagan (1986) found that when a catalyst is built from two handed pieces, a slightly lopsided supply of those pieces can give a product far more lopsided than the supply [Kagan 1986]. Soai (1995) found a reaction whose product is its own catalyst and does exactly that, so a 5% lean grows to 90% in a few rounds [Soai 1995]; by 2003 a lean of 0.00005% grew to more than 99.5% in three rounds [Sato 2003] [SB]. Established Kagan's own account of the discovery is in [Kagan 2001].

Chemistry backgrounder

TakeawayA chiral molecule is one that can't be overlaid on its mirror image, and chemists measure how lopsided a mixture of the two mirror forms is with one number, the enantiomeric excess.

A molecule is chiral when it can't be superimposed on its mirror image, the way a left hand can't be laid exactly over a right hand [SB fn 4]. The commonest cause is a stereocentre: a carbon carrying four different groups. The two mirror forms are enantiomers. In a non-chiral setting they have identical melting points, energies and reaction rates; they differ only when they meet something else that is chiral, such as an enzyme, a receptor, polarised light or another chiral molecule. Established

Labels, briefly. R/S names the 3D arrangement at a stereocentre by a fixed priority rule [SB fn 16]. D/L is the older convention still used for sugars and amino acids. Life builds proteins from L-amino acids (glycine, the simplest, isn't chiral) and nucleic acids from D-sugars [SB p. 2]. A separate label, (+)/(–), records which way a sample rotates polarised light, and that sign is not fixed by the D/L or R/S label: L-alanine, for instance, rotates light to the right in acid. So we describe life's handedness by configuration, never as “left-turning”.

A racemate is an exact 50:50 mixture of enantiomers; any other mixture is scalemic [SB fn 34]; homochiral means every molecule has the same handedness [SB fn 2]. The purity measure is the enantiomeric excess [SB fn 1]:

ee=|R−S|R+S×100%,fraction of the major form=1+ee2Eq. 0 · definition [SB fn 1]

So 90% ee is a 95:5 mixture and 50% ee is 75:25 (our arithmetic).

Diastereomers are stereoisomers that are not mirror images, for example a pair built R+R versus R+S. Unlike enantiomers, diastereomers have different energies, stabilities and reactivities. This one fact is the engine of the whole prize. Established

Catalysis speeds a reaction without the catalyst being used up. Asymmetric catalysis uses a chiral catalyst, typically a metal M bound to a chiral ligand L* (an organic molecule that binds the metal), to make one enantiomer faster than the other [SB p. 3]. Autocatalysis is when a product catalyses its own formation, so the reaction speeds up as it goes [SB].

How a chiral product comes from non-chiral ingredients. The reactions in this story add a zinc–carbon group across an aldehyde (a C=O group at the end of a molecule). The C=O carbon is flat, so the incoming group can attack from the top face or the bottom face. One face gives one enantiomer of the alcohol product, the other face its mirror image. With nothing chiral nearby the two faces are hit equally and you get a racemate; a chiral catalyst works by shielding one face [SB p. 2]. Established

Why chemists care: in drugs, often only one enantiomer has the wanted effect [PR] [POP p. 1].

The puzzle: why is life one-handed?

TakeawayIn 1953 the physicist Charles Frank showed on paper that a molecule which copies itself and sabotages its mirror image will turn a tiny random imbalance into total dominance; nobody could build one for forty years.

Abiotic chemistry without any chiral influence “invariably” gives racemates [SB p. 2], yet biology is homochiral. Something had to create an initial imbalance and something had to amplify it. Open for the actual history of life.

F. Charles Frank, a theoretical physicist at Bristol, proposed “a chemical substance which is a catalyst for its own production and an anti-catalyst for the production of its optical antimer” [Frank 1953] [SB p. 3]. Three ingredients:

  1. Autocatalysis: the product makes more of itself.
  2. Enantioselective catalysis: it makes its own handedness preferentially.
  3. Mutual antagonism: the two mirror forms suppress each other.

He ended: “A laboratory demonstration may not be impossible.” Before Kagan and Soai, (1) and (2) were each known, but (3), and (1) and (2) together, had never been shown [SB p. 3].

Why all three are needed Our illustration

A minimal Frank-type model; not Frank's notation and not in [SB]. Feed an achiral starting material A; let each enantiomer copy itself perfectly with rate constant k, and let R and S remove each other with rate constant μ:

R˙=kAR−μRS,S˙=kAS−μRSOur illustration · not in [SB]

Rewriting for the enantiomeric excess (our derivation):

ee˙=μ2(R+S)ee(1−ee2)Our illustration · ee as a signed fraction (R − S)/(R + S)

Read it like a physicist: the copying term k drops out entirely. Perfect self-copying alone only preserves the ee, and imperfect copying erodes it: a catalyst giving 90% ee makes 90%, then 81%, and so on [SB p. 10]. Amplification comes only from the antagonism term μ. Then ee = 0 is an unstable fixed point and ee = ±1 are the stable ones, so any seed, however small, runs to one hand or the other. That is the symmetry breaking Soai later saw (Tab 6).

Caveat in the committee's words: the Frank model “is not an answer to the origin of biological homochirality”; it is one possible route among several for an event 3.5–4 billion years ago [SB p. 4]. Open

Kagan 1986: when purity stops adding up

TakeawayIf the active catalyst carries two chiral ligands, the mismatched left–right pairs behave differently from the matched pairs, so product purity can be far above (or below) what the ligand's purity predicts.

Before 1986 chemists assumed product purity scaled linearly with catalyst purity [SB p. 5, Eq. 1]:

eeprod=eemax×eeligandEq. 1 [SB]

where eemax is the product ee from enantiopure ligand. That holds when the catalyst carries one chiral ligand (ML*). Kagan's group (Puchot, Samuel, Duñach, Zhao, Agami, Kagan) asked what happens when it carries two (ML*₂) [Kagan 1986] [SB p. 6]. Mix R and S ligands and three catalysts form: the matched pairs M(LR)(LR) and M(LS)(LS), which are mirror images and cancel each other out, and the mismatched heterochiral pair M(LR)(LS). The mismatched pair is a diastereomer of the matched ones; it contains one ligand of each hand (meso-like), and in Kagan's model whatever it makes is racemic.

  • If the mismatched pair is less reactive, the leftover matched catalyst is purer than the ligand pool: a positive non-linear effect (+NLE), or asymmetric amplification.
  • If it is more reactive, it floods the product with racemate: a negative non-linear effect (–NLE). Established The ± naming came from Kagan and, independently, Mikami [SB].

Kagan's model [SB Eqs. 2–3]:

eeprod=eemax·eeligand·f,f=1+β1+gβEqs. 2–3 [SB]

Here β is the ratio of heterochiral to homochiral catalyst and g is the reactivity of the heterochiral catalyst relative to the homochiral one. g = 1 gives the straight line, g < 1 bends it up (+NLE), g > 1 bends it down (–NLE) (our reading of Eq. 3). If ligands pair at random, β depends only on the ligand ee (our derivation, consistent with [POP] Fig. 4):

β=1−eeligand21+eeligand2Our derivation (random pairing)

Worked example [POP Fig. 4]: ligand 75:25 (50% ee). Random pairs: R·R 56.25%, R·S 37.5%, S·S 6.25%. If R·S barely works (g ≈ 0), the working catalyst is 56.25 : 6.25 = 90 : 10, so 80% ee from a 50% ee ligand. In Kagan's terms β = 0.6 and f = 1.6.

A limiting case the authors noted: if the inert mismatched complex forms irreversibly, product ee equals eemax whatever the ligand ee [SB p. 7]. In the ML*₂ picture product ee can't exceed eemax [SB p. 8].

The 1986 experiments [SB p. 6]: a titanium/tartrate sulfide oxidation gave –NLE; the Sharpless epoxidation of geraniol gave +NLE.

Showcase numbers [SB]

  • Noyori's DAIB ligand in diethylzinc addition to benzaldehyde: 15% ee ligand gives 98% ee product [Kitamura & Noyori 1989]. Here the mismatched zinc dimer is the most stable species and locks away the minor enantiomer, while monomers do the catalysis, a reservoir effect. Oguni had found a strong +NLE in the same reaction [Oguni 1988].
  • Merck, leukotriene antagonist L-699,392: α-pinene of 70% optical purity gave a reduction product of 95% ee [Zhao 1997].
  • Jacobsen's chromium–salen epoxide opening: the NLE showed that two metal centres act together in the selectivity-setting step, which helped establish cooperative catalysis [Hansen & Jacobsen 1996].

Later extensions [SB]: ML*₃ and ML*₄ models [Guillaneux 1994]; a hyperpositive NLE, where product ee exceeds eemax, seen so far in one system only (dimethylzinc plus benzaldehyde with N-benzylephedrine: 53% ee product at 5% ee ligand, falling to 16% at 100% ee ligand, because a mismatched dimer precipitates) [Geiger & Bellemin-Laponnaz 2020]; Blackmond's kinetic models [Blackmond 1997]; and Blackmond's demonstration that phase behaviour alone can do it: proline's racemate crystallises out, leaving a solution near 50% ee, so a +NLE appears below 50% ligand ee and a –NLE above [Klussmann & Blackmond 2006]. Reviews: [Girard & Kagan 1998] [Satyanarayana 2009].

Soai: a molecule that copies its own handedness

TakeawaySoai found a reaction whose chiral product is the catalyst for making more of itself and also obeys Kagan's amplification, so each round makes the product purer: 5% ee became 90%, and later 0.00005% became more than 99.5%.

Autocatalysis alone isn't enough. Each round of imperfect copying erodes purity (90%, then 81% …); to amplify, the catalyst must also act as Frank's “anti-catalyst” for its mirror image, which in Kagan's language means a +NLE [SB p. 10]. A precursor: Alberts & Wynberg showed “enantioselective autoinduction” (ligand ee 70% gave product ee 32%), though there the product isn't the catalyst [Alberts & Wynberg 1989].

1990, autocatalysis but erosion [Soai 1990] [SB]: diisopropylzinc (i-Pr₂Zn) added to pyridine-3-carbaldehyde, catalysed by the zinc salt of the product alcohol itself. Catalyst at 86% ee gave product at 35% ee, same handedness. Autocatalytic and selective, but purity fell. Two of Frank's three criteria.

1995, amplification [Soai 1995] [SB Fig. 7]: switch to pyrimidine-5-carbaldehyde (a six-membered ring with two nitrogens). The product, a pyrimidyl alcohol, now amplifies. Starting from 5% ee: run 1 gave 55%, run 2 gave 87%, and runs 3–5 held at a 90% plateau. All three of Frank's criteria in one flask. This is now called the Soai reaction. Established

2003, from almost nothing [Sato 2003] [SB]: with a 2-alkynyl pyrimidine (a tert-butyl-capped carbon–carbon triple bond on the ring), a seed of 0.00005% ee reached >99.5% ee in three consecutive runs. [POP] Fig. 6 gives the steps as 0.00005% → 57% → 99% → >99.5% [POP]. The committee calls this “an amplification of the stereoisomeric ratio by a factor of 630,000” [SB]. >99.5% ee means more than 99.75 : 0.25 (our arithmetic). Established

The mental picture. Each run, the zinc alkoxide product sits in the flask as catalyst. Matched clusters of the major hand work; mismatched clusters soak up the minor hand and don't. The new product inherits the majority's handedness, purer than before, and becomes next round's catalyst. Reviews: [Soai 2000] [Soai 2014].

Limits [SB p. 13–14]: it is the only known asymmetric autocatalysis that both amplifies and works with no chiral input, its substrate scope is narrow (pyrimidyl and closely related aldehydes), and it runs in dry organic solvent with organozinc reagents, so it is “not relevant for the emergence of biological homochirality in aqueous systems”.

Handedness from nothing

TakeawayWith no chiral input at all, the Soai reaction still ends up strongly one-handed, but which hand is a coin flip, exactly as Frank's model predicts for amplified random noise.

Absolute asymmetric synthesis means getting an enantiomerically enriched product with no chiral input whatever. Soai (alkyne-substituted aldehyde, R = t-BuC≡C) and Daniel Singleton (R = Me) showed it independently with the Soai reaction in 2002–03 [SB p. 13] [Singleton & Vo 2002] [Singleton & Vo 2003] [Soai 2003].

The idea: a “racemic” batch of zinc alkoxide is never exactly 50:50. Random counting fluctuation leaves a tiny excess of one hand, which Kurt Mislow called cryptochirality [Mislow 2003] [SB]. The +NLE then amplifies it. In 37 runs, 18 gave the R product and 19 gave S, with ee between 15% and 91% [SB] [Soai 2003]. That split is what a fair coin gives (our check). Established

How small is the seed? Our illustration

Not in [SB]: in N molecules of a random 50:50 mixture the typical excess is about √N, so the typical ee is about 1/√N:

eetypical≈1N,N≈6×1020(1 mmol)⇒ee≈4×10−11Our illustration · ≈ (our estimate)

That is roughly ten thousand times smaller than the deliberate 0.00005% seed of 2003 and far below anything measurable.

Other things can set the hand instead of chance [SB p. 12]: circularly polarised light shone on racemic additives, chiral crystals, minerals and surfaces, and molecules made chiral only by isotope substitution (e.g. hydrogen vs deuterium). Each tips the coin before the amplifier runs.

How does it work? (still debated)

TakeawayEveryone agrees that clusters of zinc alkoxides do the work; two detailed mechanisms disagree about which cluster and why it amplifies.

The timeline [SB p. 14–16]:

  • 1990, Soai: a zinc monoalkoxide catalyst [Soai 1990].
  • 2001, Blackmond and Brown: reaction calorimetry (measuring the heat released over time) fits a dimeric catalyst [Blackmond 2001].
  • 2003, Buono and Blackmond: kinetics fits a tetrameric transition state and predicts both rate and NLE [Buono & Blackmond 2003] [Blackmond 2004].
  • 2007, Klankermayer, Gridnev and Brown (DFT calculations, quantum-mechanical modelling of structures and energies): a square–macrocycle–square (SMS) tetramer [Klankermayer 2007].
  • 2010, Quaranta, Brown and Blackmond: the reaction gets slower as temperature rises; the active catalyst derives from SMS [Quaranta 2010].
  • 2015, Matsumoto, Kawasaki and Soai: X-ray structures of homochiral and heterochiral tetramers. Two Zn₂O₂ squares (zinc–oxygen four-rings) are joined by ring-nitrogen-to-zinc bonds into a 12-membered macrocycle, leaving two zinc atoms with open sites that can grab an aldehyde [Matsumoto 2015]. Established as structures; their catalytic role is the debate.

The two current models

  • Denmark and Houk (2020): the aldehyde docks “floor-to-floor” onto the two open zinc sites of the homochiral SMS tetramer; the heterochiral tetramer can't adopt that geometry. That is Kagan's mismatched-pair idea and Frank's antagonism, made molecular [Athavale 2020]. Evidence
  • Trapp (2020–25): in-situ mass spectrometry points instead to a short-lived zinc hemiacetal (product and aldehyde bound together) as the real catalyst. Monomer and dimers exist, but the dimers act as a kinetic gate, not a trap; amplification is a kinetically controlled non-equilibrium steady state, which the committee calls “different from the mechanism put forward by Kagan and Frank” [Trapp 2020] [Möhler 2025] [SB]. Evidence

[SB]: “Two detailed mechanisms have been put forward.” They may both be right for different substrates, since Denmark and Trapp studied different aldehydes [Geiger 2022]. Open

Why it matters, what's open, sources

TakeawayNon-linear effects became a standard tool for reading catalytic mechanisms, and the Soai reaction is the proof that one-handedness can arise by chemistry alone, though not how life did it.

  • A mechanism probe. Plot product ee against catalyst ee: a straight line is consistent with one chiral unit in the selectivity-setting step; a curve signals two or more, aggregation, or a phase effect. This is now routine in asymmetric catalysis (Jacobsen's two-metal discovery is the classic case) [SB p. 7, 9, 16] [POP p. 6]. Established
  • A design lever. Chiral poisoning (Faller: adding a cheap chiral additive to switch off one hand of a racemic catalyst) [Faller & Parr 1993] and chiral activation (Mikami) [Mikami 1997] let chemists get high ee without an enantiopure catalyst [SB p. 9]. Established
  • Process chemistry. Merck's 70% → 95% ee step for L-699,392 [SB]. Carreira's group made a key intermediate of the anti-HIV drug efavirenz by an asymmetric autocatalytic step, though no NLE data were reported [Chinkov & Carreira 2011] [SB p. 14]. Broader claims of impact on pharmaceuticals, flavours, agrochemicals and materials come from [POP]/[PR] without named examples [POP] [PR]. Evidence for those general claims.
  • Origin of life. A proof of concept that homochirality can emerge from achiral ingredients in a closed system, the first laboratory demonstration of Frank's model [SB p. 13]. It is not the prebiotic route; current work looks at reaction networks and kinetic resolution in water [Deng, Yu & Blackmond 2024]. Open

Established

  • Product ee can bend away from catalyst ee in both directions when catalysts pair or aggregate.
  • Asymmetric autocatalysis with amplification exists (1995).
  • 0.00005% → >99.5% ee in three runs (2003).
  • Absolute asymmetric synthesis with random handedness (Soai and Singleton, independently).

Evidence

  • The detailed Soai mechanism (SMS tetramer vs zinc hemiacetal) [SB].

Open

  • How biology's homochirality actually arose ([SB]: “we will probably never have a definitive answer”) [SB].
  • A Soai-like amplifier for amino acids or sugars in water ([POP] p. 6 says groups are trying) [POP].
  • Wider industrial use of autocatalysis.
