The 2026 Prize in Economic Sciences: building macroeconomics from the bottom up

Robert E. Hall and Michael Woodford, shared equally, 12 Oct 2026

The prize

TakeawayHall showed that a forward-looking theory of household spending survives a sharp test in the data, and Woodford built on it a complete, small model of output, inflation and interest rates that central banks now use to set policy.

On 12 October 2026 the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences awarded the Sveriges Riksbank Prize in Economic Sciences in Memory of Alfred Nobel, shared equally, to Robert E. Hall (born 1943, Palo Alto; PhD MIT 1967; Stanford University) and Michael Woodford (born 1955, Chicopee, Massachusetts; PhD MIT 1983; Columbia University) “for microeconomic foundations of macroeconomic stabilisation” [PR]. The prize is 12 million SEK [PR].

“The laureates' work has provided us with a stable theoretical foundation for the models that are now used by central banks and governments around the world. With them, we are better equipped to deal with shocks in the economy, such as energy crises or sudden changes in how much households consume.” John Hassler, committee chair [PR]

The problem the prize is about

Economies alternate between downturns and upturns. Stabilisation policy (the central bank's interest rate and the government's taxes, transfers and spending, used to damp those swings) needs a model that answers a counterfactual: if we cut the rate by one percentage point now, what happens to output and inflation over the next two years? Until the 1970s that model was a set of statistical relations between aggregates, such as “consumption is two thirds of disposable income” and “inflation is low when unemployment is high”. In the 1970s those relations shifted, and policy built on them produced inflation without lower unemployment [SB p. 1, 9] [PR].

The prize rewards the two steps that rebuilt the model from the decisions of individual households and firms, its microfoundations:

  • Hall (1978). If a household plans its spending with rational, forward-looking expectations, then its future change in consumption cannot be predicted from anything it knows today. That turns an untestable theory (expectations are not observed) into a regression anyone can run. In US aggregate data, the prediction largely held [Hall 1978] [SB p. 3, 18]. With Mishkin (1982) he then measured how many households do not behave this way: about 20% [Hall & Mishkin 1982]. Established
  • Woodford (1990s, book 2003). He assembled the New Keynesian model: Hall's consumer, firms that set prices but cannot change them every period, and a central bank that sets an interest rate by a rule, with no role for the money stock. Its simplest form is three equations [Woodford 2003] [SB p. 5, 25]. It gave policy a welfare criterion, a theory of why interest-rate rules pin down the price level, and the case for communicating future policy (forward guidance). Established

The two contributions were made independently and years apart; the committee also stresses that the final framework uses work by many others, including Taylor, Rotemberg, Calvo, Blanchard and Kiyotaki, and the late Julio Rotemberg as Woodford's main co-author [SB p. 3–4].

The old model and why it broke

TakeawayThe Keynesian model predicts a large multiplier because it assumes households spend a fixed two thirds of each extra unit of income; the Lucas critique says such a coefficient is not a constant of nature but a function of how policy behaves, so it shifts when policy changes.

The Keynesian consumption function and the multiplier

Let Y be output (real GDP), C private consumption, I investment, G government purchases and T net taxes, all real flows per period. The economy is closed. The Keynesian model assumes output is set by demand (firms have idle capacity), so Y=C+I+G. Consumption is postulated, not derived, as an affine function of disposable income Y−T [SB eq. 1–2]:

C=α+γ(Y−T),α>0,  γ∈(0,1).

The parameter γ is the marginal propensity to consume (MPC): the share of an extra unit of income spent now.

Derivation · the multiplier

Substitute C into Y=C+I+G: Y=α+γY−γT+I+G. Collect Y:

Y=11−γ(α+I+G−γT)⟹∂Y∂G=11−γ,−∂Y∂T=γ1−γ.

The first derivative is the spending multiplier. The second is the multiplier of a transfer cheque (a cut in T): only the fraction γ of the cheque is spent in the first round, so it is smaller by the factor γ. The amplification comes from the geometric series 1+γ+γ2+⋯=1/(1−γ): each round of spending is someone's income, of which the share γ is spent again.

Fitted to data, γ≈2/3, which gives a spending multiplier of 3 and a cheque multiplier of 2 [SB p. 7, fn. 7]. Everything therefore depends on γ. Fig. 2 plots both multipliers against γ. The marker at the left is the MPC that Hall's forward-looking consumer has out of a one-off income change, r/(1+r)≈0.02 at a 2% real rate (derived in tab 3): at that MPC both multipliers are close to their minimum, 1 and 0.

Money, interest and the Phillips curve

Hicks (1972 laureate) closed the model with the IS curve (investment falls with the nominal interest rate i, so the multiplier equation gives output as a decreasing function of i) and the LM curve, money-market equilibrium M‾/P=L(Y,i), where M‾ is the money supply set by the central bank, P the price level, and L the demand for real money balances, increasing in Y and decreasing in i [SB p. 7–8]. With P fixed in the short run, a larger M‾ lowers i and raises Y. How prices move was a separate block: the Phillips curve, a stable inverse relation between inflation and unemployment (Phillips 1958), read as a menu: lower unemployment could be bought with higher inflation [SB p. 8].

The 1970s and the Lucas critique

In the 1970s the West had high unemployment and high inflation at the same time (stagflation); the Phillips curve appeared to shift outward [SB p. 9]. Lucas (1995 laureate) argued that this was not bad luck but a flaw of the method [Lucas 1976]. The coefficients of the Keynesian equations are not “deep”: they summarise how people respond to the policy environment they have seen. Change the policy, and the coefficients change.

The Lucas critique, formally Let the economy be described by structural parameters Θ (preferences, technology) and a policy rule with parameters Φ. A reduced-form regression of one aggregate on another estimates a coefficient b=b(Θ,Φ). Using b to predict the effect of a new rule Φ′ assumes b(Θ,Φ′)=b(Θ,Φ), which is false whenever agents' decisions depend on their forecasts of policy. A model is robust to the critique if it is written in terms of Θ and solved again for each Φ.

A concrete case, previewing tab 3 Our derivation: suppose households follow Hall's rule and the government changes taxes by shocks that persist with autocorrelation ρT. An econometrician who measures how much consumption moves per unit of surprise tax change then finds an MPC of r/(1+r−ρT). Under a regime of one-off tax rebates (ρT=0) it measures about 0.02; under a regime of permanent tax changes (ρT=1) it measures 1. The “MPC” in the Keynesian function is a property of the tax policy, not of households. This is the precise sense in which the 1970s relations were bound to shift once policy tried to exploit them [SB p. 9–10].

Lucas, Prescott, Sargent and Wallace called for models built from explicit optimisation with “deep” parameters. The first such model, the real business cycle (RBC) model of Kydland and Prescott (2004 laureates), had flexible prices and perfect competition, so it left no role for stabilisation policy [Kydland & Prescott 1982] [SB p. 10]. The task the laureates took on was to keep the RBC model's discipline and put back the market failures that make stabilisation useful.

Hall I: the forward-looking consumer

TakeawayAn optimising household equates the marginal value of a unit spent today with the expected value of saving it; with quadratic utility this makes planned consumption flat, so consumption moves only when news about lifetime income arrives, and by an amount set by how persistent that news is.

The household's problem

A household plans from period t to t+T. In period t+τ it consumes ct+τ≥0, earns labour income wt+τ (random: the only source of uncertainty), and holds financial wealth at+τ that earns a constant real interest rate r>0. It discounts future utility by β∈(0,1). The period utility function u is strictly increasing and strictly concave, so an extra unit of consumption is worth less the more one already consumes (diminishing marginal utility u′). 𝔼t is the expectation given everything known at t. The problem is [SB eq. 3–6]:

max{ct+τ}⁡ 𝔼t∑τ=0Tβτu(ct+τ)s.t.at+τ+1=(1+r)(at+τ−ct+τ+wt+τ),at+T+1≥0.

The terminal condition forbids dying in debt. Because u is increasing, it binds, and iterating the budget forward gives one lifetime constraint: the present value of consumption equals initial wealth plus the present value of earnings [SB eq. 7],

∑τ=0Tct+τ−wt+τ(1+r)τ=at.

Hall did not invent this problem; it formalises Friedman's permanent-income and Modigliani's life-cycle ideas with rational expectations. His contribution was the implication he drew from it and how he took it to data [SB p. 13].

The Euler equation

Derivation · a perturbation argument

Take an optimal plan and change it slightly: consume δ less at t, save it, and consume the proceeds (1+r)δ at t+1. Every constraint still holds. The utility cost today is u′(ct)δ; the expected discounted gain tomorrow is β(1+r)𝔼tu′(ct+1)δ. At an optimum (with consumption away from zero) no such change can raise expected utility, so cost equals gain:

u′(ct)=β(1+r)𝔼tu′(ct+1).(E)

Repeating the argument over τ periods gives the committee's form, u′(ct)=(1+r)τβτ𝔼tu′(ct+τ) [SB eq. 8].

Equation (E), the stochastic Euler equation, says marginal utility is planned to change at the constant rate β(1+r). If β(1+r)=1, the household plans to keep marginal utility, and hence consumption, flat, whatever the expected profile of earnings: consumption smoothing. Two things are absent from (E): the level of wealth and the path of earnings. That absence is what Hall tested (tab 4).

The theory separates anticipated and unanticipated income changes. A worker told today that they will be laid off in three months should cut spending today, and should not cut it again when the pay stops [SB p. 15]. Fig. 3 shows this case.

A closed form: quadratic utility

Take β(1+r)=1 and quadratic utility u(c)=−12(φ−c)2, where φ is a “bliss” level never reached, so u′(c)=φ−c is linear [SB p. 15].

Derivation · the consumption function

With linear u′ and β(1+r)=1, (E) becomes 𝔼tct+1=ct, so 𝔼tct+τ=ct for all τ. Take 𝔼t of the lifetime constraint and use ∑τ=0T(1+r)−τ=(1+r−(1+r)−T)/r:

ct=r1+r−(1+r)−T(at+wt+𝔼t∑τ=1Twt+τ(1+r)τ).

This is the committee's eq. (9) [SB eq. 9]. As T→∞ the factor in front tends to r/(1+r).

So consumption is a constant share r/(1+r) of wealth plus the expected present value of earnings (permanent income). The MPC out of a one-off windfall is r/(1+r): at r=2% per year that is 0.0196≈1/50, against 2/3 in the Keynesian function. As the committee puts it, whether the MPC is 1/50 or 2/3 decides whether fiscal stimulus works [SB p. 16].

How much does consumption respond to news?

Suppose earnings follow an autoregression around a mean w‾: wt−w‾=ρw(wt−1−w‾)+et, with 𝔼t−1et=0 and persistence ρw∈[0,1]. A shock e that dies out fast (ρw near 0) is transitory; one that never dies out (ρw=1) is permanent.

Proposition · MPC out of news Our derivation
Under the assumptions above with T→∞, ct+1−ct=r1+r−ρwet+1.
Proof

Because 𝔼tct+1=ct, the change ct+1−ct equals the surprise ct+1−𝔼tct+1. Wealth at+1 is fixed at t by the budget, so the only surprise in ct+1 is the revision of expected earnings, (𝔼t+1−𝔼t)wt+1+τ=ρwτet+1. Hence

ct+1−ct=r1+r∑τ=0∞ρwτ(1+r)τet+1=r1+r⋅11−ρw/(1+r)et+1=r1+r−ρwet+1.∎

The limits check the formula: ρw=0 gives r/(1+r), the windfall MPC; ρw=1 gives 1, so a permanent raise is spent in full. At r=0.02: ρw=0.9 gives 0.167 and ρw=0.95 gives 0.286. Consumption tracks permanent income. This is the result the Lucas-critique example in tab 2 used.

Hall II: the random-walk test and what it found

TakeawayHall replaced “predict consumption from expectations we cannot see” with “show that nothing known today predicts tomorrow's change in consumption”; aggregate US data largely passed, and the failures, measured by Hall and Mishkin, became a number: about one household in five lives hand to mouth.

Why the theory looked untestable

The consumption function of tab 3 depends on 𝔼t∑wt+τ/(1+r)τ, a forecast no one observes. Before 1978 researchers proxied it with ad hoc averages of past income (“distributed lags”), which are not the rational forecast except for special income processes (Muth 1960) and are exposed to the Lucas critique [SB p. 11–12]. Worse, for any observed ct one can invent expectations that rationalise it, so the level equation cannot be rejected [SB p. 16].

Hall's move: test the change, not the level

Derivation · from the Euler equation to a regression

Replace the expectation in (E) by the realised value minus a forecast error ϵt+1, with 𝔼tϵt+1=0 [SB eq. 10]:

u′(ct+1)=(β(1+r))−1u′(ct)+ϵt+1.

With u′(c)=φ−c this is linear in consumption [SB eq. 11]:

ct+1=φ(1−(β(1+r))−1)+(β(1+r))−1ct−ϵt+1.

With β(1+r)=1 it collapses to 𝔼tct+1=ct [SB eq. 12].

A process with 𝔼tct+1=ct is a martingale, the general form of a random walk. The testable content is an orthogonality condition: for any variable xt known at t (lagged income, wealth, interest rates), 𝔼[ϵt+1xt]=0. So Hall regressed ct+1 on ct, a constant, and lagged variables xt, and tested whether the coefficients on xt are zero [SB p. 17]. Unobserved expectations drop out: they are summarised in ct.

The criterion inverted the usual logic of empirical work. A good fit normally supports a theory; here, a failure to predict was the success [SB p. 18]. The committee draws the parallel with the efficient-markets hypothesis (Fama, 2013 laureate), with one difference: in stock markets, arbitrageurs remove predictability; no one arbitrages household consumption, so its unpredictability was a stronger surprise [SB p. 18].

Result. In quarterly US aggregate data, after controlling for current consumption, disposable income and any number of its lags had minimal predictive power for nondurable consumption. Stock-market values had some, moderate, predictive power, which the pure theory does not allow [Hall 1978] [SB p. 18–19]. Established

The hand-to-mouth extension

Hall also stated what a failure would look like. Suppose a fraction μ of households cannot borrow and consume their disposable income y each period (hand to mouth), while the rest follow the Euler equation. With quadratic utility and β(1+r)=1, per-capita consumption obeys [SB eq. 13]

ct+1−ct=μ(yt+1−yt)−(1−μ)ϵt+1.

The first term is the hand-to-mouth group's change in spending, the second the optimisers' surprise. Now 𝔼t(ct+1−ct)=μ𝔼t(yt+1−yt): whenever income changes are predictable, consumption changes are predictable too, in proportion μ. That is excess sensitivity, and the size of the predictable part measures μ.

The interest-rate response and a caveat

Hall (1988) let the real rate rt vary and used constant-relative-risk-aversion utility u(c)=(c1−σ−1)/(1−σ). Without uncertainty the Euler equation ct−σ=β(1+rt)ct+1−σ gives, after taking logs, Δln⁡ct+1=1σ(ln⁡β+ln⁡(1+rt)). The slope of consumption growth on the interest rate is 1/σ, the intertemporal elasticity of substitution (IES). Hall's estimate, about 0.1, means interest rates move planned consumption only weakly [SB p. 21]. The same σ reappears as the coefficient 1/σ in the New Keynesian IS curve of tab 6, which is how Hall's measurement enters Woodford's model.

Zeldes (1989) added a caveat: with CRRA utility the martingale holds for marginal utility, not for consumption, and the precautionary saving that uncertainty induces can by itself create apparent excess sensitivity [Zeldes 1989] [SB p. 20]. The quadratic case imposes certainty equivalence (the amount of income risk does not affect spending), which is why its tests are a benchmark and not the last word.

Hall's rule-of-thumb households are the ancestor of the heterogeneous-agent models (Huggett 1993, Aiyagari 1994) and of today's HANK models, New Keynesian models with uninsurable income risk where MPCs differ across households [SB p. 25, 41].

Woodford I: firms that set prices

TakeawayGive firms market power and let each reset its price only occasionally; then a firm that resets must forecast future costs and prices, and aggregating its choice gives a Phillips curve in which today's inflation depends on expected inflation and on how far output is from the level flexible prices would deliver.

Woodford's strategy was to start from the RBC model and change one thing: prices are sticky, meaning they are not adjusted every period to clear markets. To make “setting a price” meaningful, firms need some monopoly power [SB p. 27]. The simplest model has no capital, no government and no foreign trade.

Many goods, one price index

A continuum of goods i∈[0,1] enters the household's consumption basket through the Dixit–Stiglitz aggregator, with elasticity of substitution ε>1 between goods [SB p. 31–32]:

Ct=(∫01Ct(i)ε−1εdi)εε−1,Pt=(∫01Pt(i)1−εdi)11−ε,Ct(i)=Ct(Pt(i)Pt)−ε.
Derivation · demand and the price index

Minimise spending ∫P(i)C(i)di subject to the aggregator equalling C. With multiplier λ, the first-order condition for good i is P(i)=λC(i)−1/εC1/ε, so C(i)=C(P(i)/λ)−ε. Substituting into the aggregator gives 1=λε(∫P(i)1−εdi)ε/(ε−1), so λ=P as defined above. Then ∫P(i)C(i)di=PC: P is the cost of one unit of the basket, the exact price index.

The flexible-price benchmark

Firm i produces with labour only, Y(i)=AH(i), where A is productivity and H hours. Its nominal marginal cost is W/A (W the nominal wage).

Derivation · the markup

Facing demand Y(i)=C(P(i)/P)−ε, the firm maximises (P(i)−W/A)C(P(i)/P)−ε. Setting the derivative in P(i) to zero: 1−ε(P(i)−W/A)/P(i)=0, so

P(i)=εε−1⏟μWA.

The problem has a maximum only if ε>1 [SB p. 32].

With flexible prices all firms are identical, so W/P=A/μ: the real wage is below the marginal product of labour by the markup μ>1. The household's labour-supply condition is C−σ(W/P)=χH1/ψ, where ψ is the Frisch elasticity of labour supply and χ a scale constant [SB p. 31].

Natural output and the natural rate Woodford defined natural output Yn as the equilibrium with flexible prices, and the natural real rate rn as the real rate in that equilibrium. With χ=1 [SB fn. 35]: Ytn=μ−ψψσ+1Atψ+1ψσ+1,1=β(1+rtn)(At+1At)−σψ+1ψσ+1.
Proof

Use C=Y=AH and W/P=A/μ in the labour-supply condition: (AH)−σA/μ=H1/ψ, so Hσ+1/ψ=A1−σ/μ and H=(A1−σ/μ)ψ/(1+σψ). Then Y=AH=μ−ψ/(1+σψ)A1+ψ(1−σ)/(1+σψ), and the exponent of A simplifies to (1+ψ)/(1+σψ). The natural rate follows from the Euler equation with Ct=Ytn in each period: 1=β(1+rtn)(Yt+1n/Ytn)−σ. ∎

Two features matter later. Natural output is a purely real object: no money or prices appear. And a productivity rise that is expected to fade, At+1<At, lowers rtn: goods are plentiful today relative to tomorrow, so their price in terms of tomorrow's goods falls [SB p. 29, 33]. The output gap is y~t=ln⁡Yt−ln⁡Ytn, the distance from the efficient (flexible-price) level, a notion the committee credits to Woodford [SB p. 27] [POP p. 5].

Calvo pricing

In Calvo's (1983) device, each period each firm may reset its price with probability 1−θ, independently across firms and over time; otherwise its price stays fixed [Calvo 1983]. A price set today survives s more periods with probability θs, so the expected duration of a price is 1/(1−θ) periods. Because the firms that reset are a random sample, the prices that do not change have the same distribution as last period's prices, which gives the index recursion [SB eq. 18]:

Pt1−ε=(1−θ)(Pt*)1−ε+θPt−11−ε,

where Pt* is the price chosen by all firms that reset at t (they face the same problem). A firm that resets maximises expected discounted profits over the periods its price may last [SB p. 34]:

maxPt*⁡ 𝔼t∑s=0∞θsQt,t+s(Pt*Yt+s|t−Wt+sAt+sYt+s|t),Yt+s|t=Ct+s(Pt*Pt+s)−ε,

with Qt,t+s=βs(Ct+s/Ct)−σPt/Pt+s the household's nominal discount factor [SB fn. 37]. This is the forward-looking firm: it raises its price today if it expects costs or competitors' prices to rise while the price is stuck.

The New Keynesian Phillips curve

Write lower-case letters for logs, πt=pt−pt−1 for inflation from t−1 to t, and mc^t for the log deviation of real marginal cost (Wt/Pt)/At from its flexible-price value 1/μ. Linearise around a steady state with zero inflation.

πt=β𝔼tπt+1+κy~t,κ=(1−θ)(1−βθ)θ(σ+1ψ).
Proof

Step 1 (index). Linearising the recursion: pt=θpt−1+(1−θ)pt*, so πt=(1−θ)(pt*−pt−1).

Step 2 (reset price). The first-order condition of the reset problem is 𝔼t∑sθsQt,t+sYt+s|t(Pt*−μWt+s/At+s)=0. To first order around the zero-inflation steady state the weights Qt,t+sYt+s|t are proportional to βs, so

The reset price is a weighted average of future nominal marginal costs (times the markup), with weights (βθ)s: further-off periods count less because of discounting and because the price may have been reset by then.

Step 3 (combine). Subtract pt−1 and use 𝔼tpt+1*−pt−1=𝔼t(pt+1*−pt)+πt:

By step 1, pt*−pt−1=πt/(1−θ) and 𝔼t(pt+1*−pt)=𝔼tπt+1/(1−θ). Multiply through by (1−θ): πt=(1−θ)(1−βθ)mc^t+(1−θ)πt+βθ𝔼tπt+1, so θπt=(1−θ)(1−βθ)mc^t+βθ𝔼tπt+1. Divide by θ.

Step 4 (marginal cost and the gap). In logs, labour supply gives wt−pt=σct+ht/ψ; with ct=yt and ht=yt−at, real marginal cost is wt−pt−at=σyt+(yt−at)/ψ−at. With flexible prices it equals the constant −ln⁡μ at yt=ytn. Subtracting, mc^t=(σ+1/ψ)(yt−ytn)=(σ+1/ψ)y~t. ∎

Read the curve forward: iterating it gives πt=κ∑j≥0βj𝔼ty~t+j. Inflation today is the discounted sum of expected future output gaps, because resetting firms price in the marginal costs they expect while their price is stuck. The old Phillips curve related inflation to current unemployment; this one makes expectations of future demand pressure part of today's inflation. That is why central-bank credibility and communication matter in this model [POP p. 5].

The slope κ rises as prices become more flexible (θ falls) and as marginal cost becomes more sensitive to output (σ+1/ψ rises). The limit θ→1 (prices never change) gives κ→0, a flat curve, and θ→0 gives κ→∞, the flexible-price case where any gap is inflated away at once.

Note on the committee's text. The Scientific Background prints the slope as (1−β)(1−βθ)(1/ψ+σ)/θ [SB p. 36]. That expression does not vanish when prices never change: at θ=1 it equals (1−β)2(σ+1/ψ). The derivation above, and the standard references [Woodford 2003] [CGG 1999], give (1−θ) in place of (1−β). With β=0.99, θ=0.75, σ=1, 1/ψ=1, the two forms give 0.1717 and 0.0069, a factor of 25. We read the printed form as a typographical error.

The three-equation model

TakeawayHall's Euler equation becomes the demand block, Calvo pricing the supply block, and an interest-rate rule closes the model; the rule pins down a unique equilibrium only if it raises the nominal rate more than one for one with inflation in the long run.

Equation 1: the New Keynesian IS curve

Derivation · from Hall's Euler equation [SB p. 35]

In equilibrium Ct=Yt. Without uncertainty the Euler equation with a nominal bond is Yt−σ=β1+it1+πt+1Yt+1−σ. Divide both sides by (Ytn)−σ and use the definition of the natural rate, 1/(1+rtn)=β(Yt+1n/Ytn)−σ:

(YtYtn)−σ=(Yt+1Yt+1n)−σ1+it1+πt+111+rtn.

Take logs, use ln⁡(1+x)≈x, and divide by −σ. Under uncertainty the same steps hold state by state; averaging over states adds 𝔼t (certainty equivalence of the linear system) [SB fn. 38].

y~t=𝔼ty~t+1−1σ(it−𝔼tπt+1−rtn).(IS)

The output gap falls when the expected real rate it−𝔼tπt+1 is above the natural rate, by an amount set by Hall's 1/σ. Unlike the old IS curve it is dynamic and contains expected inflation [SB p. 28]. Technology does not enter directly: it moves output only through rtn.

Equation 2: the New Keynesian Phillips curve

πt=β𝔼tπt+1+κy~t.(PC)

Derived in tab 5. The committee's text calls πt “inflation between t−1 and t+1” [SB p. 28]; with its own definition πt+1≡(Pt+1−Pt)/Pt, πt is inflation from t−1 to t, the timing used here.

Equation 3: the interest-rate rule

it=ρ+ϕππt+ϕyy~t+vt,ρ=1β−1.(R)

The central bank sets the nominal rate, raising it when inflation or the gap rises; vt is an unexpected policy move (positive is contractionary) with mean zero; ρ is the steady-state real rate [SB eq. 16, p. 36]. Taylor (1993) proposed such a rule and showed it fitted US policy [Taylor 1993]. There is no money stock anywhere. Woodford showed this is not a gap: money serves only as the unit in which prices are sticky, and the system is the limit of a monetary economy as the value of money balances goes to zero, with M/P→0 through M→0 at a finite P [Woodford 1998] [SB p. 38–39]. This shifted monetary analysis from money-supply targets to interest-rate rules, the way most central banks now operate [SB p. 4–5].

When does a rule pin down the price level?

An old objection (Sargent and Wallace 1975): if the central bank just sets i, real balances M/P are determined but M and P separately are not, so the price level is indeterminate [SB p. 39]. In (IS)–(PC)–(R) no variable is predetermined, so a bounded solution is unique only if the system has no stable forward root.

Proposition · determinacy (the Taylor principle) ([Bullard & Mitra 2002], [Woodford 2003, ch. 4])
With ϕπ,ϕy≥0, the model has a unique bounded equilibrium if and only if κ(ϕπ−1)+(1−β)ϕy>0.
Proof Our derivation

Set the shocks to zero and substitute (R) into (IS); with Ω≡σ+ϕy+κϕπ the system is zt=A𝔼tzt+1 for zt=(y~t,πt)′ and

A=1Ω(σ1−βϕπσκκ+β(σ+ϕy)),det⁡A=βσΩ,tr⁡A=σ+κ+β(σ+ϕy)Ω.

Both variables are free to jump, so the only bounded solution is z=0 exactly when both eigenvalues of A lie inside the unit circle; otherwise bounded “sunspot” paths exist. For a real 2×2 matrix, both roots of λ2−(tr⁡A)λ+det⁡A lie inside the unit circle iff |det⁡A|<1 and |tr⁡A|<1+det⁡A. Here 0<det⁡A<1 because Ω≥σ>βσ, and tr⁡A>0. The remaining condition tr⁡A<1+det⁡A reads σ+κ+βσ+βϕy<σ+ϕy+κϕπ+βσ, which simplifies to κ(ϕπ−1)+(1−β)ϕy>0. ∎

Interpretation: a permanent rise in inflation by dπ raises the gap by (1−β)dπ/κ in the long run (from PC), so the rule raises i by (ϕπ+ϕy(1−β)/κ)dπ. The condition says this exceeds dπ: the nominal rate moves more than one for one, so the real rate rises and leans against the inflation. With ϕy=0 it reduces to ϕπ>1, the form the committee states [SB p. 29, 39]. The result is local; paths that leave the neighbourhood of the steady state can still be equilibria (Benhabib, Schmitt-Grohé and Uribe 2001), and the fiscal theory of the price level is an alternative route to determinacy [SB fn. 46, p. 40]. Established

Solving the model

The committee solves a one-period policy shock: vt>0 at t only. From t+1 on everything is back at steady state, so 𝔼ty~t+1=𝔼tπt+1=0, and eliminating πt and it gives y~t=−vt/(σ+ϕπκ+ϕy) and πt=κy~t: a surprise rate rise lowers output and inflation, so monetary policy has real effects [SB p. 37]. Shocks that persist need the general solution.

Proposition · persistent shocks Our derivation
Let vt=ρvvt−1+ηt and r^tn≡rtn−ρ=ρrr^t−1n+ζt. Under determinacy the solution is y~t=(1−βρ)Λ(ρ)(r^tn−vt),πt=κΛ(ρ)(r^tn−vt), Λ(ρ)=1(1−βρ)(σ(1−ρ)+ϕy)+κ(ϕπ−ρ), with ρ=ρv for the policy shock and ρ=ρr for the natural-rate shock.
Proof

Guess y~t=axt, πt=bxt for xt=−vt (the r^n case is identical, since only vt−r^tn enters). Then 𝔼txt+1=ρxt. (PC) gives b=βρb+κa, so b=κa/(1−βρ). (IS) with (R) gives σa(1−ρ)=−ϕπb−ϕya+ρb+1. Substitute b and solve: a=(1−βρ)Λ, b=κΛ. With ρ=0, a=1/(σ+ϕy+κϕπ), the committee's one-period formula. ∎

The two shocks enter only through r^tn−vt. A rise in the natural rate (households turn optimistic, or productivity is expected to grow) acts like an unexpected rate cut; a fall in the natural rate acts like a rate rise. Fig. 6 solves the model for any parameters you choose.

Policy: what the model changed

TakeawayBecause the model says which shock moved output, it can recommend opposite responses to two falls in output; because agents look ahead, announcing future policy moves the economy today, so much that the simplest model overstates it.

Same fall in output, opposite advice

The old framework read a fall in output as a call for stimulus. In the model, the right response depends on whether the gap or natural output moved [SB p. 29, 37–38]:

  • A demand shock (households turn pessimistic, r^tn<0): natural output is unchanged, actual output and inflation fall, the gap is negative. The rule cuts the rate. Established
  • A productivity rise expected to fade: natural output rises, rn falls, and output rises by less than natural output because many firms cannot cut prices, so the gap falls. The rule cuts the rate, helping output catch up with its higher natural level [SB p. 37].
  • An energy-price hike (lower productivity, expected to recover): natural output falls by more than actual output, because firms that cannot reset do not price in the higher costs. The gap is positive and inflation rises. The rule raises the rate and lowers output further. The committee calls this a central-bank action “that may appear destabilizing” but is sensible stabilisation [SB p. 38] [POP p. 5].

Fig. 6 (energy shock, default calibration) gives the numbers Our derivation: productivity −1% with persistence 0.9 lowers natural output by 1.00%, raises the natural rate by 0.10 percentage points per quarter, and produces a gap of +0.085%, so output falls by 0.915%; quarterly inflation rises by 0.13 percentage points and the policy rate by 0.21 (0.54 and 0.85 at annual rates). The committee credits this logic with better responses to recent supply shocks than in the 1970s [POP p. 5]. Evidence

The divine coincidence, and when it fails

Proposition Our derivation (named by Blanchard and Galí 2007; [SB p. 41–42, fn. 52])
If the rule tracks the natural rate, it=rtn+ϕππt+ϕyy~t (no vt), then πt=y~t=0 for all t is an equilibrium, and under the Taylor principle it is the only bounded one.
Proof

Substitute π=y~=0: (PC) holds as 0=0; in (IS) the bracket is rtn+0−0−rtn=0, so it holds too; (R) gives it=rtn. Uniqueness: the deviation system is the homogeneous system of the determinacy proposition. ∎

In this model stabilising inflation and stabilising the efficient gap are the same goal: there is no trade-off. The coincidence fails once wages are also sticky, or with a cost-push shock (a disturbance in the Phillips curve itself, part of Woodford's 2003 baseline), and policy then faces a real trade-off between inflation and the gap [SB p. 42, fn. 53].

A welfare criterion

Because the model is built from a household's utility, policy can be ranked by that utility, which the old framework could not do [SB p. 29–30]. Woodford showed that a second-order approximation of household welfare around the efficient steady state is, up to scale,

ℒ=𝔼0∑t=0∞βt(πt2+κεy~t2)

[Woodford 2003, ch. 6] (stated, not derived on this page). Inflation is costly here not for the usual reasons but because, with staggered prices, it spreads relative prices of identical goods apart, so households buy inefficiently unequal amounts. With ε=6 (a 20% markup) and κ=0.17, the weight on the gap is 0.029: one point of quarterly inflation costs as much as about six points of output gap, since 1/0.029≈5.9. This is one theoretical basis for inflation targeting. Woodford also showed that a rule like (R) is optimal under some conditions, and, against the time-inconsistency problem of Kydland and Prescott (1977), argued for policy chosen from a “timeless perspective” that converges to a time-invariant rule [SB p. 42].

Forward guidance and its puzzle

Iterate (IS) forward, assuming the gap returns to zero eventually:

y~t=−1σ∑j=0∞𝔼t(it+j−πt+j+1−rt+jn).

Today's gap depends on the whole expected path of real-rate gaps, not just today's rate. So when the policy rate is stuck at its lower bound, a central bank can still stimulate by promising low rates later. Woodford and others proposed this forward guidance, and it was used during the zero-lower-bound period, in the US usually dated 2008–2015 [SB p. 38]. It works only if the promise is believed. Woodford (2005) argued that transparency about goals and methods is itself a policy instrument [SB p. 47].

The same formula shows the problem. A one-quarter cut of the real rate by x, k quarters from now, raises today's gap by x/σ whatever k is, and by (PC) raises today's inflation by κ(x/σ)∑j=0kβj=κ(x/σ)(1−βk+1)/(1−β), which grows with k Our derivation. A promise about ten years ahead is more powerful than a cut today. Del Negro, Giannoni and Patterson found such “unreasonably strong” effects of far-off announcements in the model; this is the forward guidance puzzle. Several amendments have been proposed and none is a consensus [Del Negro et al. 2023] [SB p. 46]. Open

Fiscal policy in the same model

Add government spending, taxes, transfers and debt and the model evaluates fiscal policy; finance ministries and international organisations use such versions [SB p. 42]. Two lessons. A transfer that raises demand also raises inflation, and an independent central bank with an inflation mandate raises rates and offsets part of the stimulus [SB p. 5]. And because MPCs differ (tab 4), transfers aimed at high-MPC households can be more effective than monetary policy [SB p. 47]. Hall's later work also concerned the labour market not part of the citation: Hall (2005) argued that wage stickiness amplifies unemployment fluctuations [Hall 2005]. That paper does not appear in the committee's documents; we include it only because it is often mentioned in coverage of the prize.

From three equations to a central-bank model

Practical models add capital, sticky wages, habits, indexation and more shocks: Smets and Wouters' estimated models have 7 shocks [Smets & Wouters 2007] [SB fn. 47]; the DYNARE software (Michel Juillard, 1994) solves and estimates them [SB fn. 55]. Rotemberg and Woodford (1997) estimated an early version and used its utility-based welfare to rank rules [RW 1997]. Sveriges Riksbank's model MAJA (Corbo and Strid 2020) is an open-economy version for Sweden, where about half of demand comes from exports; it has Calvo prices, Calvo wages set by unions, a smoothed interest-rate rule, and 29 exogenous shocks, 16 of them domestic [SB p. 43–44].

Has it helped?

The committee's verdict is hedged. The rebound after the Great Recession was faster than after the Great Depression (Eichengreen and O'Rourke 2010), and the post-2021 inflation burst, which the committee compares with the 1973 oil shock, was not followed by 1970s-style stagflation (Ari et al. 2023; Forbes et al. 2024; Blanchard and Bernanke 2024; Forbes et al. 2025). But the shocks differ in kind and size, so “it is hard to prove beyond reasonable doubt” that better policy caused the faster recoveries, although “there does seem to be a consensus that they have played a very important role” [SB p. 47–48]. Evidence One date in the committee's text is inconsistent: page 47 dates the recent inflation burst from 2021, page 48 says the shock “began in 2025”; the studies cited (2023–2025) can only concern the earlier episode, so we use 2021.

Limits, open questions, sources

TakeawayThe model's strength is that every equation comes from stated preferences, technology and frictions; its weak points are the frictions chosen for convenience, the linearisation, and the assumption that everyone's expectations follow the model.

What the model captures and what it misses

  • Linearisation. The model is solved around a steady state. Nonlinearities such as the zero lower bound are missed, and the bound affects behaviour even in normal times. Global solution methods have advanced a lot [SB p. 45]. Evidence
  • Rational expectations and anchoring. Agents' expectations agree with the model. After a burst of energy prices the model predicts slow convergence back, but it cannot rule out that people start to fear runaway inflation; it presumes expectations stay anchored at the target. Rare, hard-to-predict events (war, pandemic) are outside it [SB p. 45–46]. Open
  • Ad hoc price stickiness. Calvo and Rotemberg pricing are not derived from deeper primitives. With large shocks firms should change prices more often. Models with fixed (menu) costs exist, for example Golosov and Lucas (2007), but there is no new consensus model [SB p. 46]. Open
  • The forward guidance puzzle (tab 7). Open
  • Heterogeneity. The representative household has one MPC; data say MPCs differ widely. HANK models address this and connect directly to Hall and Mishkin's work [SB p. 41]. Evidence
  • Scope. The model is about responding to shocks, not preventing them; the 2008 crisis was, on most analyses, a job for regulation [SB p. 48].

Established

  • The Euler-equation (random-walk) test and its orthogonality logic.
  • Aggregate consumption changes are hard to predict; household data show excess sensitivity.
  • The three-equation model, its derivation, and the Taylor principle.
  • Interest-rate rules without money as the frame for monetary policy.

Evidence

  • Size of the hand-to-mouth share (0.2 to 0.5 depending on data).
  • That NK-based policy explains faster recoveries since 2008.
  • Better responses to supply shocks than in the 1970s.

Open

  • The forward guidance puzzle.
  • A microfounded replacement for Calvo pricing.
  • When inflation expectations de-anchor.
